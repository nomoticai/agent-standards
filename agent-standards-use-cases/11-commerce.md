# 11. Commerce (CMC)

## Scope, status, and terminology

This document is a problem statement, not a solution proposal. Its subject is the **coordination and evidence layer of agent value transfer**: machine-readable offers at discovery time, purchase authority under delegation, transaction records both parties hold, economics that survive machine volumes, and disputes resolvable from structured evidence. It argues that these properties are unmet by human-centric commerce infrastructure — and, unusually for this suite, that the industry has begun shipping partial answers while fragmenting exactly as document 01 predicted. It stops short of prescribing an offer format, a mandate encoding, or a settlement mechanism, and it explicitly does **not** propose a payment network: the claim throughout is a layer that *composes with* existing and future rails, not one that replaces them.

**Scope discipline, stated up front.** Cross-currency execution, tax-jurisdiction attribution, escrow and staged release, revenue-share across composed workflows, and subscription semantics under changing delegations are real and are *deferred*: they are extensions atop the fundamentals, and folding them into the core statement dilutes it. This document receives one seam from tools (10) — the commercial dimension of cross-organization tool sharing: metering, usage attribution, and payment terms ride on the fundamentals stated here — and hands its evidence forward to audit (12).

## The skeptical case

Commerce infrastructure is among the most mature on the internet. Card networks clear billions of transactions; ACH, SEPA, and wires move value at every scale; gateways expose developer-grade APIs; e-commerce platforms run global storefronts; ISO 20022 structures enterprise payments; open-banking APIs initiate transfers; stablecoin networks offer programmable settlement. An agent is an application: give it credentials and a gateway API, and it can charge, refund, and reconcile like any other software. Nothing new required.

The skeptic's position has hardened since this suite's earlier drafts, because the industry has begun building the agent layer itself: agent-checkout protocols from the major platforms, mandate-based authorization schemes from the card-network ecosystem, and HTTP-native micropayment settlement with real transaction volume. The updated skeptical case is no longer "use Stripe" — it is "the agentic commerce stack is being built by the parties who own the rails; a problem statement is arriving after the solutions."

## Where the mismatch actually lies

The in-flight industry work is addressed squarely below, because it is this document's best evidence in both directions. First, the underlying mismatches — which that work confirms rather than obviates. Every layer of deployed commerce assumes a human present at the moment of decision: catalogs formatted for eyes, checkout flows expecting clicks, authentication regimes expecting the cardholder, receipts formatted for reading, disputes expecting narrative. Agents transact at machine speed and volume, across organizational boundaries, on behalf of principals absent at transaction time. Five specific gaps follow:

**Offers at discovery time.** Human commerce prices the product page; the human has already committed attention. Agent selection needs machine-readable, merchant-attributable offers — price, terms, availability — *in the discovery result*, so candidates are compared before attention is committed. Without signed offers, the agent is scraping human catalogs or consuming per-vendor APIs, and comparison is the manual normalization failure of document 06 with money attached. *(Joint with discovery.)*

**Authority, not just credential.** Deployed payment verification asks one question: is the credential valid? The agent case requires a second: does this agent hold authority for *this purchase* on *this principal's* behalf, within what bounds? That is documents 03 and 04 arriving at the payment step — chain lineage and mandate reference at the moment money moves — and its absence is why an agent with a stored card number is indistinguishable, to every deployed rail, from the cardholder.

**Evidence both parties hold.** Gateway receipts live in the gateway's format, in the gateway's systems, disputed through the gateway's process. When the counterparty denies the transaction, each side holds partial, provider-resident records. Agent commerce at volume requires offer, acceptance, and confirmation as signed records held by both parties, verifiable independently of any provider — the paper-trail property of document 03's purchase orders, generated by the transaction rather than reconstructed after it.

**Machine-scale economics.** Percentage-plus-fixed-fee pricing fits human-sized purchases and fails at fractional-cent, high-frequency transfers — data queries, computation calls, per-step workflow fees. This gap is real and old, and this document states it with its history attached (below), because "the fees are too high" was the pitch of thirty years of failed micropayment systems, and the honest version of the claim is different from the fee complaint.

**Disputes from structure.** Chargeback regimes assume a human narrating what went wrong to a provider applying its own rules. Agent disputes need to begin from the signed record: what was offered, what was accepted, what was confirmed — with the honest limit, developed below, that structure covers the *agreement* and fulfillment retains a residue.

**The in-flight protocols, read honestly.** As of this writing, the agentic commerce stack is arriving in layers: agent-to-merchant checkout protocols from the major platform players (product feeds, cart construction, purchase triggering), mandate-based authorization schemes backed by the card-network ecosystem (cryptographically signed records of what the principal authorized the agent to spend, and under what limits), Visa- and Mastercard-side trusted-agent efforts, and HTTP-native stablecoin settlement reviving the 402 status code for machine-to-machine micropayments — the last with nine-figure transaction counts within months of launch. Two readings, both correct. *Validation:* the layers map almost one-to-one onto the five gaps above — checkout protocols answer discovery-time offers, mandate schemes answer delegated authority and evidence (using, notably, the literal vocabulary of document 04), 402-revival answers machine economics. The parties who own the rails do not build protocols for imaginary problems; the gaps are confirmed by the responses. *Fragmentation:* there are now competing stacks at each layer, backed by rival platform coalitions, with practitioners advised to design for multi-protocol interoperability — document 01's N-stacks dynamic arriving in commerce in real time, at the layer where translation errors cost money directly. The problem statement's role is therefore not to precede the solutions but to name what any of them, or their eventual convergence, must satisfy — and what none of them can promise.

## What observably breaks today

**Agent purchasing is scraping plus stored cards.** The deployed pattern is an agent parsing human catalogs (or per-vendor feeds), holding card credentials as if it were the cardholder, and clicking through checkout flows built for humans — with the credential indistinguishable from the principal and the authority chain existing nowhere.

**The budget exists only at the issuer.** A delegated spending cap is enforced, if anywhere, at grant time; no merchant can see it, no cross-vendor consumption is tracked, and — inheriting document 03's finding without re-litigating it — the "$500 minus already spent" figure that selection logically requires is shared mutable state that no offline credential can carry. The commerce layer does not escape that trilemma; it is where the trilemma gets expensive.

**Receipts don't reconcile to authority.** Finance holds a card charge and a merchant invoice; neither references the delegation, the mandate, or the cap — document 03's reconciliation archaeology, which that document established and this one inherits at the transaction layer.

**Micropayments remain structurally unserved on traditional rails.** Fee floors make sub-cent transfers irrational on card infrastructure; the emerging 402-based settlement handles the digital-goods and API case and is stablecoin-bound, regulatorily unsettled, and unsuited to physical fulfillment — a partial answer, not a resolution.

**Disputes run on provider process.** Each party's evidence is resident in systems that may no longer be motivated to cooperate; reconstructing offer → acceptance → fulfillment is cross-provider archaeology under each provider's rules.

**The law assumes a human.** Strong-customer-authentication regimes legally *require* human presence at authentication for many transactions; card-network liability rules classify fraud by cardholder presence and have no category for "credential present, human absent by design"; consumer chargeback rights for agent-initiated purchases are unresolved; and money-transmission and tax treatment of machine-scale settlement are open. These are not implementation details — they are standing constraints any solution must be shaped around.

## Properties without a common definition

Framed as what buyers, merchants, and later auditors must be able to establish:

- **Comparable offers before commitment:** machine-readable, merchant-signed offer terms available at discovery, so selection precedes attention. *(Joint with 06; offer claims are evidence per 05's framing — attested versus asserted, not scored.)*
- **Authority at the moment of payment:** the merchant can verify the mandate and chain behind the purchase, not merely the credential — with document 03's receiver questions (scope, bounds, currency, instance) applying to the payment act verbatim, and the cap-enforcement point *named* (issuer-partitioned, online-checked with defined latency, or post-hoc with defined exposure) rather than implied solved.
- **Agreement as dual-held evidence:** offer, acceptance, and confirmation as signed records held independently by both parties, referencing the mandate, verifiable without any provider's cooperation. *(Expressed in 09's acts; bound via 07's envelope; consumed by 12.)*
- **Merchant identity worth verifying:** the selling party as an identified entity under document 02's properties, so offer signatures attach to something — with trustworthiness remaining 05's evidence question, not an identity byproduct.
- **Economics that admit machine volume:** composition with settlement mechanisms whose costs don't forbid the transaction sizes agents rationally generate — stated as a composition requirement, not a mandate that the substrate clear value.
- **Disputes that begin from the record:** resolution procedures that consume the dual-held evidence — with the fulfillment residue explicit (below).
- **Regulatory expressibility:** the human-authentication, liability-classification, and consent constraints representable in the transaction's records, so compliance is a property of the flow rather than a retrofit.

## Prior art, and what it constrains

**The micropayment graveyard** disciplines the economics claim. DigiCash, Millicent, CyberCash, and the W3C micropayment efforts all pitched sub-cent transfers against fee floors, and all died; HTTP 402 sat reserved and unused for some thirty-five years as the standing monument. The honest post-mortem is Szabo's: the binding constraint was never only fees but *mental transaction costs* — human attention doesn't scale down to the denomination. That analysis contains the one genuinely new variable: agents are the first buyers with no attention cost. The early nine-figure volumes on 402-revival settlement are the first evidence that the graveyard's premise has changed — and the claim must still be stated as "the human-side barrier is removed," not "micropayments are now easy," because the regulatory and settlement-finality barriers remain.

**EDI and the purchase-order regime** (via documents 03 and 09) are the deployed precedent for authority-carrying, paper-trailed B2B transactions — at human cadence, with per-pair implementation residue; the PO/invoice/approval chain is the institutional model for the evidence layer, and its bilateral-guide history is the ceiling reminder. **ISO 20022** shows structured payment semantics achieving adoption at treaty pace.

**The card networks' own machinery** is prior art in both directions: 3-D Secure and strong-customer-authentication regimes are deployed authentication *built to require the human* — the constraint in institutional form; network chargeback rules are the deployed dispute system whose categories the agent case escapes; PCI-DSS is why the emerging pattern keeps payment credentials *out* of the agent's context entirely — an architectural lesson (the agent coordinates; the credential lives elsewhere) that any solution should be measured against, since an agent context holding card numbers is both a compliance violation waiting and document 10's injection surface with a payout attached.

**The in-flight agentic protocols** are the nearest prior art and the live fragmentation exhibit: checkout-layer protocols (platform-coalition-backed, with major retailers), mandate-based authorization (the card-network coalition's answer to documents 03/04, adopting the mandate vocabulary outright), trusted-agent attestation efforts from the networks, and 402-native settlement. Their layered structure — offer/checkout, authorization, settlement — is itself a finding this document adopts as scope discipline; their multiplicity per layer is document 01's lesson landing where translation costs are denominated in currency; and their existence sets the bar for any further proposal: state the delta against the shipping stack, or be presumed part of it.

**The vending machine**, kept from earlier drafts with its limit attached: it demonstrates merchant-side automation, published prices, and deterministic transaction — under *simultaneous exchange*: payment and fulfillment complete together, so there is no fulfillment gap. Most agent commerce has the gap, and the gap is where trust, escrow, and disputes actually live; the analogy licenses the offer-and-payment step and is silent past it. The **barcode-and-register** image survives as the machine-readable-offer illustration it is.

The constraint the history imposes: offers, authority, evidence, and settlement each have deployed or in-flight instances; nothing yet provides their combination portably across the competing stacks, under the legal constraints named above, with evidence independent of any provider — and the graveyards (micropayments for three decades; provider-resident evidence in every chargeback fight) mark the failure modes any convergence must escape.

## The core use case: an assistant purchasing supplies within a delegated budget

An organization grants an assistant agent authority to spend up to $500 per month on office supplies for a department — deliberately the same authority scenario as document 03, because this document is that scenario's transaction layer and must not contradict its findings. The assistant must discover suppliers, compare offers, verify a counterparty, transact under the mandate, and leave records finance can reconcile and either side can dispute from.

**What breaks today.** Discovery is per-vendor: Shopify storefront APIs here, a proprietary catalog there, HTML for humans elsewhere — comparison by parsing, with nothing signed. Payment runs through whichever gateway each supplier integrated, so the assistant either holds credentials across gateways (a PCI problem and an injection-with-payout surface) or adds an orchestration layer (another integration point). The supplier sees a valid credential and nothing else: no principal, no mandate, no cap — and *cannot* see remaining budget, because per document 03 that figure is shared state no credential carries; today it is not merely unenforced downstream but unrepresented anywhere outside the grantor. The receipt is the gateway's format; finance parses it and cannot connect it to the delegation. When the wrong items arrive, the dispute runs on the merchant's policy and the gateway's chargeback rules, from records each party holds only partially.

**What any adequate answer must make possible.** Offers from multiple suppliers, signed and comparable, at discovery. Counterparty identity verifiable (02), with offer claims carrying provenance under the department's own evidence policy (05). The purchase act (09) carrying the mandate reference (04) and chain (03), so the supplier verifies authority — and the cap's enforcement point is *named*, with its exposure window, rather than assumed. Acceptance and confirmation as signed, dual-held records referencing all of the above, so finance reconciles in one query and a dispute begins from a shared account of the agreement.

**What no answer can promise, and this document does not claim.** Enforcement of the cumulative cap by credential alone — document 03's trilemma governs, and this document's contribution is only that commerce is where its cost lands. Evidence of fulfillment: the dual-held record proves what was offered, accepted, and confirmed; whether the right goods arrived in the right condition is an off-substrate fact (document 05's oracle problem in physical form), so structured evidence resolves agreement disputes and *bounds* — not eliminates — fulfillment disputes. Regulatory transcendence: strong-customer-authentication law, chargeback classification, and consumer-protection rights for agent purchases are open constraints; a solution owes representable compliance, not a workaround. Settlement itself: this layer coordinates and evidences; the rails move the money.

**Open questions this use case exposes.** Where the cap's enforcement point should live for delegated retail purchasing; whether agent purchases carry the principal's chargeback rights; and how dual-held evidence interoperates across the competing in-flight stacks — solution-space and legal-space; the failures stand regardless.

## Additional commerce needs

Stated as observable needs or failures; deferred extensions are listed separately.

**Selection**
- Compare offers across merchants at discovery time from signed, machine-readable terms rather than scraped catalogs. *(Joint with 06.)*
- Distinguish attested merchant claims (identity, terms, availability) from asserted ones at selection time. *(05's evidence framing.)*

**Authority and payment**
- Present, and let the merchant verify, the mandate and chain behind a purchase at the moment of payment. *(03/04 at the payment act.)*
- Keep payment credentials out of the agent's execution context while the agent coordinates the transaction. *(PCI lesson; 10's injection surface.)*
- Transact at sizes and volumes where per-transaction fee floors currently forbid the exchange, by composition with suitable settlement. *(With the graveyard's honesty attached.)*

**Evidence and disputes**
- Hold, on both sides, independently verifiable records of offer, acceptance, and confirmation, referencing the mandate. *(07/09 mechanics; 12 consumption.)*
- Reconcile a charge to its authorizing delegation in one query. *(03's core case, discharged at this layer.)*
- Begin a dispute from the shared agreement record, with the fulfillment residue explicit.
- Detect and evidence a merchant's terms changing between offer and confirmation.

**Deferred extensions** *(real, and out of core scope)*: cross-currency execution and rate binding; tax-jurisdiction attribution; escrow and staged release; revenue-share attribution across composed workflows *(the fuller form of 10's metering seam)*; recurring payments across delegation changes.

## What this document does not claim

- **Not** a payment network. The claim is a coordination and evidence layer composing with rails — card, bank, stablecoin, and whatever follows — and language implying replacement of the settlement layer is disclaimed outright.
- **Not** priority over the in-flight industry work. The shipping protocols are treated as partial answers and as evidence; the claim is the property set any of them, or their convergence, must satisfy — and the fragmentation among them is itself part of the problem statement.
- **Not** an escape from document 03's trilemma. Cumulative caps require a named enforcement point with a defined exposure window here exactly as there.
- **Not** that structured evidence resolves fulfillment. The record covers the agreement; delivery is an off-substrate fact with a permanent dispute residue.
- **Not** that micropayments are solved. The human-side barrier (attention cost) is removed by the buyer being an agent; fee, finality, and regulatory barriers are live.
- **Not** a legal position. The SCA collision, chargeback classification, consumer-protection scope, and money-transmission questions are named as constraints, not answered.

The claim is: agent value transfer requires machine-readable offers at discovery, verifiable authority at payment, dual-held evidence of agreement, economics admitting machine volume, and disputes that begin from records — properties human-centric infrastructure does not natively provide; the industry's own in-flight protocol layers confirm each gap while fragmenting across rival stacks exactly as this suite's first document predicts; and until the property set has a common definition across those stacks, agent commerce remains per-integration scraping with stored credentials, unrepresented budgets, provider-resident evidence, and disputes by archaeology — at the one layer where every translation error has a denomination.
